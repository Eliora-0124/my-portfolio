Course Reflections & Feedback

A space to document my learning journey, track my weekly progress, and reflect on course feedback.

Week 1

This week introduced us to digital practices and the relationship between technology and society. Instead of viewing technology in isolation, I learned to think critically about how digital media both shapes and is shaped by our social context. It’s a great starting point for exploring digital media technology through both theory and practical work this semester.

Week 2

This week focused on website structures, HTML/CSS basics, and publishing models. Coming from a background in App UI design, trying out CampusPress and GitHub for web creation was a completely new and challenging experience, but I will keep learning by combining AI and classroom tutorials. I ultimately chose GitHub to build my portfolio. Through this process, I also realized that I need to be more rigorous with my visual and textual layout details, and I will continue to explore and refine them.

Week 3

This week, we explored the basics of web scraping, learning how to transform unstructured web content into structured, analyzable data (in CSV format) using pre-made tools such as browser extensions. During the practical workshop, I experienced the entire process of harvesting web data using the Web Scraper extension and successfully integrated the dataset and visual results into my personal portfolio. However, I also found that writing custom Python scripts for web scraping can be quite challenging and time-consuming, as it involves dealing with complex libraries and environmental setups. Therefore, I chose to gain a fundamental understanding of Python-based scraping without planning to dive deeply into it right now. By comparison, utilizing existing visual tools to understand the underlying data structures of digital media proved to be much more efficient and intuitive for me.

Week 4

During the process of collecting and analyzing Reddit data using Communalytic, our team experienced a "sense of intrusion" regarding user privacy. Students posting about topics such as "AI cheating anxiety," "fear of academic misconduct," or "exam stress" shared their experiences under the expectation of anonymous community support, rather than anticipating becoming research subjects. To resolve this ethical dilemma while adhering to data protection and research standards, we implemented strategies of "De-identification" and "Qualitative-to-Quantitative Mapping": 1.Stripping Personal Identifiers: We completely removed all user IDs, exact timestamps, and direct URLs. 2.Converting Text to Numeric Data: By applying our 5-variable encoding dictionary, we transformed subjective, personal narrative posts into objective numeric codes (e.g., 1, 1, 3, 3, 1). This aggregated individual private experiences into broader, anonymized behavioral patterns. This reflection highlighted that data analysis goes beyond technical scraping and coding—it requires fundamental respect for data subjects. In future research, we will maintain strong ethical awareness to ensure public data is handled legally, responsibly, and with full privacy protection.

Week 5

In the workshop, we explored how data can be collected, organised and presented through visualisations. Our group created a dataset about the main barriers people experience when using AI and visualised 31 responses in a pie chart. The results showed that fear of policy violations and academic integrity was the most common barrier, with 15 responses (48.4%). This was followed by no clear barrier, with 9 responses (29%). Fabricated references or hallucinations received 3 responses, while lack of depth and other barriers each received 2. During my undergraduate studies, I had worked on data visualisation before, but I mainly thought it involved finding data and using design to create an attractive and creative chart. This workshop helped me realise that data visualisation is also about interpreting data, communicating findings and considering how audiences understand the information. I found the pie chart useful because it made the differences between categories easy to understand. However, the chart initially displayed only category numbers, without explaining their meanings. This made me realise that visualisations need clear labels and should be designed with the audience in mind. Feigenbaum and Alamalhodaei (2020) explain that data visualisation is not simply about presenting facts. Choices such as colours, categories and layout can influence how people understand information. Feminist data visualisation also encourages us to consider whose experiences are included or excluded and whether the data contains bias. I also looked at The Pudding's article Who Gets Shipped and Why? (Cai et al., 2024). I found its interactive visualisations effective because they allowed readers to explore different relationships in fanfiction. However, focusing on popular pairings may mean that less common relationships receive less attention. Overall, this workshop and reading helped me understand that data visualisation involves more than choosing a chart type. It requires critical thinking about data, representation, bias and the audience.

Week 4

1. Data Collection and Manual Scraping I collected the most recent 15 social media posts from 32 friends, giving me a total of 480 posts. I used Sumpter's 13 categories to classify each post, assigning each post to only one category and recording the number of posts in each category in an Excel spreadsheet. I found the process of manual scraping quite time-consuming because I had to look through every post individually and make decisions about its meaning. Some posts contained several elements at the same time. For example, a photograph might show someone's outfit, a meal with friends and a travel location. I had to decide which category best represented the main purpose of the post. This made me realise that data collection is not simply about recording information. It also involves human interpretation and judgement. Different researchers might classify the same post differently. 2. Problems with the Categories and Classification Although Sumpter's 13 categories provided a useful starting point, I found that some categories were not always sufficiently clear or specific. One particular difficulty was distinguishing between selfies and Lifestyle posts. I often classified selfies under Lifestyle because they were usually part of someone's daily life. However, I also realised that a separate category for selfies could create further problems. For example, if someone posted a photograph of themselves alongside a record of their daily activities, it was difficult to decide whether the post should be classified as a selfie or Lifestyle. This made me question whether creating more categories would necessarily make the classification more accurate. Instead, I think it is important to establish clearer classification rules. For example, if the main purpose of a post is to show someone's appearance or personal style, it could be considered self-presentation. If the main purpose is to record a daily activity or experience, it could remain under Lifestyle. I also noticed that some categories, such as Politics/News and Local Events, appeared less frequently in my collected data, while Lifestyle, Jokes/Memes and Product/Advertising appeared more often. This suggests that the original categories do not necessarily reflect the same patterns across different groups of users. 3. The Influence of Life Stages and Occupations One of the most interesting things I noticed was that people's social media content seemed to be related to their life stages and occupations. For friends who were still studying, I noticed more posts about everyday activities, travel and leisure. This might be partly because students often have more opportunities to travel and share experiences during holidays. In contrast, friends who had started working seemed to share fewer posts about eating out and leisure activities. When they did share social activities, these were sometimes photographs of gatherings or meals with friends. I also noticed that some of their work-related posts expressed frustration or dissatisfaction with their jobs, which I found quite interesting. I also looked at several beauty influencers and noticed a different pattern. Their posts contained considerably more product advertisements and promotional content. They also frequently participated in social events and activities. I think this may be related to their professional responsibilities, as promoting products, attending events and maintaining an online presence are part of their work. These observations suggest that social media content is influenced not only by personal interests but also by people's life stages, occupations and social circumstances. However, these are observations from a limited sample rather than conclusions that can be generalised to everyone. 4. Iterating and Improving Sumpter's Method After completing the initial classification, I realised that the main problem was not necessarily the number of categories, but the lack of clear boundaries between some of them. Therefore, I would improve the method by keeping the original 13 categories but developing more specific rules for classifying ambiguous posts. For example: If a post mainly focuses on someone's appearance, outfit or personal image, I would consider its self-presentation aspect when deciding its category. If a photograph mainly documents a daily activity, such as travelling, eating or spending time outdoors, I would classify it according to the main activity or context. If a post contains several different elements, I would assign it to the category that best represents its main purpose, rather than counting it in multiple categories. I would also keep brief anonymous notes about difficult cases so that I could apply the same rules consistently. This iteration would help reduce inconsistent decisions and make the classification process more transparent. However, it would not completely remove subjectivity because deciding the main purpose of a post still requires interpretation. 5. What Does This Iteration Tell Me, and Why Is It Important? This process showed me that the way data is categorised can significantly influence the results. If I change the classification rules, the distribution of posts across categories may also change. Therefore, the patterns shown in a spreadsheet or graph are partly shaped by the researcher's decisions. It also made me realise that categories are not neutral. They determine which aspects of people's online activities become visible and which aspects might be overlooked. For example, grouping selfies and daily-life photographs together under Lifestyle might hide the importance of personal appearance and self-presentation. On the other hand, separating every type of content into a different category could make the classification complicated and inconsistent. This is important because social media platforms also collect and categorise user data to create profiles and make predictions about users. If their categories are too broad or their interpretations are inaccurate, the resulting profiles may not reflect users' actual identities. 6. Can 15 Social Media Posts Accurately Represent Someone's Identity? I do not think that 15 social media posts can fully represent a person's identity. Social media only shows the parts of life that people choose to share. Many aspects of someone's personality, emotions, relationships and everyday experiences may not appear online. However, I do think that these posts can reveal certain aspects of a person's online identity. For example, they can provide clues about someone's interests, lifestyle, occupation, social relationships and the image they want to present to others. My observations about students, working adults and beauty influencers also suggest that online content is shaped by people's different life circumstances and professional roles. Therefore, I would argue that social media data can provide a partial picture of someone's identity, but it should not be treated as a complete or entirely accurate representation of who they are. Overall, this activity helped me understand that algorithmic identity is not simply discovered from existing data. It is also constructed through the ways data are collected, selected, categorised and interpreted. The process of turning people's online activities into numerical categories may make their identities easier to analyse, but it can also oversimplify the complexity of real human lives.
